# Tour de France 2011 — Cadel Evans' Historic Victory

**Category:** Society & Economics

**Date:** July 24, 2011 (race conclusion)

## Summary

The 2011 Tour de France marked a watershed moment in cycling history when Australian cyclist Cadel Evans claimed victory over Luxembourg's Andy Schleck in one of the closest margins in the race's modern era. Evans' triumph on July 24, 2011, at the conclusion of the three-week, 3,430-kilometre race made him the first Australian-born rider to win the Tour de France in its 108-year history. Riding for BMC Racing, Evans edged Schleck by just 1 minute 34 seconds, with Andy's brother Frank Schleck rounding out the podium in third place at +2:30—the first time two brothers shared a Tour de France podium. The 2011 edition unfolded amid dramatic circumstances, including Thomas Voeckler's unexpected ten-day defence of the yellow jersey, Andy Schleck's audacious long-range solo attack over the Col du Galibier on Stage 18, and Evans's decisive ride in the penultimate-day individual time trial that overturned Schleck's slender lead.

## Significance

Cadel Evans' victory represented a breakthrough for Australian cycling and resonated deeply across the cycling world and beyond. Prior to 2011, no Australian had ever won the world's most prestigious cycling race, despite the sport's growing popularity in the Southern Hemisphere. Evans, at age 34, achieved the feat after a distinguished career spanning nearly two decades, during which he had previously finished second in the 2008 and 2009 Tours. His win exemplified the value of perseverance and tactical acumen in a sport increasingly dominated by powerful professional teams and sophisticated training methods.

The 2011 Tour also produced one of the era's most celebrated attacking rides. On Stage 18, Andy Schleck launched a solo bid roughly 60 kilometres from the finish, soloing over the Col d'Izoard and the Col du Galibier to win the stage and close on the overall lead. Evans, rather than waiting for rival teams to organise a chase, spent much of the final climb driving the pursuit himself to limit his losses—a decision widely credited with saving his Tour. The contrast between Schleck's romantic attack and Evans's disciplined, calculating defence framed much of the race's drama.

The victory bolstered Australian cycling's global profile, inspired a new generation of Australian cyclists, and demonstrated that success in the Tour de France remained attainable for riders outside the traditional cycling powerhouses of Western Europe.

## Key Figures

- **Cadel Evans** (BMC Racing) — 34-year-old Australian winner; total time 86:12:22; first Australian champion
- **Andy Schleck** (Leopard Trek) — Luxembourg runner-up at +1:34; previously runner-up in 2009 and 2010; won Stage 18 with a long solo attack over the Galibier
- **Frank Schleck** (Leopard Trek) — Andy's brother; third overall at +2:30; the pair were the first brothers to share a Tour de France podium
- **Thomas Voeckler** (Europcar) — French rider who defended the yellow jersey for ten days through the Pyrenees and Alps before ceding it on Stage 19; finished fourth overall
- **Samuel Sánchez** (Euskaltel-Euskadi) — sixth overall and King of the Mountains (polka-dot jersey) winner; 2008 Olympic road-race champion
- **Mark Cavendish** (HTC-Highroad) — green jersey (points classification) winner; won five stages, including the closing sprint on the Champs-Élysées
- **Pierre Rolland** (Europcar) — white jersey (best young rider) winner; won Stage 19 atop Alpe d'Huez

## Narrative Arc

**Early Stages (July 2–8)**
The 2011 Tour de France began on July 2 with a road stage from Passage du Gois to Mont des Alouettes in the Vendée, western France, won by the Belgian Philippe Gilbert (Omega Pharma–Lotto), who took the first yellow jersey. Stage 2, a team time trial around Les Essarts, went to Garmin–Cervélo, moving Thor Hushovd into the lead. Mark Cavendish (HTC-Highroad) opened his sprint account in the opening week en route to five stage wins and the green jersey. Evans and BMC Racing focused on steady, safe progress through the flat stages, staying near the front and avoiding the crashes that felled several rivals. On Stage 9, Thomas Voeckler (Europcar) seized the yellow jersey from a breakaway and would defend it far longer than anyone expected. The race's character shifted decisively when it reached the Pyrenees in the second week.

**Mountain Stages & Leadership Shifts (July 9–18)**
Through the Pyrenees, Voeckler clung to the yellow jersey while the overall contenders — Evans, the Schleck brothers, Alberto Contador, and Samuel Sánchez — tested one another on the climbs. Sánchez won atop Luz-Ardiden and Jelle Vanendert took the summit finish at Plateau de Beille, but the general-classification favourites finished closely grouped, leaving Voeckler narrowly ahead as the race turned toward the Alps. Evans repeatedly marked the decisive accelerations, staying within reach of the podium without wasting energy on doomed attacks.

**Andy Schleck's Galibier Raid (July 21, Stage 18)**
Stage 18 — Pinerolo to the Col du Galibier (Serre Chevalier), 200.5 km — became the emotional high point of the 2011 Tour. Andy Schleck launched a bold solo attack roughly 60 kilometres from the finish, crossing the Col d'Izoard alone and pressing on over the Galibier, at 2,645 m the highest stage finish in Tour history to that date. The move threatened to blow the race apart. Evans, seeing that no rival team would organise a chase, moved to the front of the yellow-jersey group and drove the pursuit almost single-handedly up the Galibier to limit his losses — a ride widely credited with saving his Tour. Schleck won the stage, but Thomas Voeckler dug deep to retain the overall lead by just 15 seconds; Schleck moved up to second, with Evans still firmly in contention.

**The Alpe d'Huez and the Decisive Time Trial (July 22–24)**
Stage 19 climbed to Alpe d'Huez, where Pierre Rolland attacked to win the stage and secure the white jersey. Voeckler finally cracked under the pressure, and Andy Schleck took the yellow jersey with a slender advantage over his brother Frank and Cadel Evans heading into the decisive individual time trial. On Stage 20, a 42.5-km test against the clock in Grenoble, Evans produced one of the finest time trials of his career, finishing more than two and a half minutes faster than Andy Schleck and overturning the deficit to seize the yellow jersey by 1 minute 34 seconds. The final stage on July 24 was the traditional ceremonial procession into Paris, where Mark Cavendish won the sprint on the Champs-Élysées and Evans was crowned champion, with Andy and Frank Schleck completing the first all-brothers Tour podium.

## Legacy

The 2011 Tour de France symbolized several turning points in professional cycling. Evans' victory at 34 years old challenged prevailing assumptions that Tour champions must be in their late twenties, showing that experience and mental toughness could overcome youthful athleticism. His win opened doors for Australian cycling, inspiring riders like Richie Porte to pursue Tour contention in subsequent years.

The race is also remembered for the tactical contrast at its heart: Andy Schleck's romantic, long-range attacking style against Cadel Evans's calculating, all-round consistency and time-trial strength. Evans's willingness to chase on the Galibier rather than wait for rivals, followed by his commanding time trial in Grenoble, came to exemplify how modern Grand Tours are won as much by disciplined damage-limitation as by spectacular attacks.

The 2011 Tour's media narrative globally shifted cycling's center of gravity: beyond its traditional strongholds in France, Belgium, and Italy, the sport was becoming increasingly global, with Australian, Korean, Colombian, and African riders expanding the sport's reach and geographic diversity.

## Broader Sporting Significance in 2011

Evans' Tour victory arrived in the middle of a remarkable summer of international sport. Rory McIlroy had won the U.S. Open the month before (June 19) with a record-breaking score; the FIFA Women's World Cup ran concurrently with the Tour's final stages; and the Rugby World Cup would crown New Zealand in October. Evans joined a cohort of champions who in 2011 redrew the map of global athletic excellence — Australian, Northern Irish, and Japanese (Women's World Cup) athletes reaching the pinnacle of their sports in the same calendar year.
