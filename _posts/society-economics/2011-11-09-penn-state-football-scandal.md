## Summary

On **November 5, 2011**, Penn State University campus police arrested **Jerry Sandusky**, a former assistant football coach, on **40 counts** of child sexual abuse spanning more than a decade. The charges alleged that Sandusky — who had founded The Second Mile, a charity serving at-risk youth in 1977 — had systematically abused boys he met through the charity and through his continued access to Penn State athletic facilities after his retirement in 1999.

The arrest triggered the immediate disclosure that in **February 2002**, graduate assistant **Mike McQueary** had witnessed Sandusky sexually abusing a young boy in the Penn State football team's shower facilities and had reported what he saw to head coach **Joe Paterno** the following morning. Paterno reported the matter to athletic director **Tim Curley** and senior vice president **Gary Schultz**, neither of whom alerted law enforcement or child protective services.

On **November 9, 2011** — four days after the arrest — Penn State's Board of Trustees forced Paterno's resignation, ending his **46-year tenure** as head coach and the winningest career in major college football history. University president **Graham Spanier** was also dismissed the same night. The twin firings marked the most dramatic collapse of a collegiate sports institution in American history.

## Background: Jerry Sandusky and The Second Mile

Sandusky had served as Penn State's defensive coordinator under Paterno from **1969 to 1999**, earning a national reputation as one of college football's most innovative defensive strategists. His defenses helped Penn State win **two national championships** (1982 and 1986) and produced multiple NFL players.

In **1977**, Sandusky founded **The Second Mile**, a Pennsylvania-based nonprofit that served emotionally troubled, at-risk youth. The charity grew to serve approximately **100,000 youth** annually by the early 2000s and received significant public recognition — including a 1990 letter of commendation from President George H.W. Bush as one of the **1,000 Points of Light**. The charity provided Sandusky with a pool of vulnerable young boys over whom he cultivated positions of trust and authority, a structure that investigators would later describe as the operational foundation of his abuse.

Sandusky retired from coaching in **1999** at age 55 — a departure that, in retrospect, Penn State officials acknowledged came with unusual conditions, including Sandusky retaining access to Penn State football facilities and locker rooms. Internal university documents later revealed that Paterno had urged Sandusky to retire rather than face what was already known within the program about his behavior.

## The 2002 Locker Room Incident

The pivotal event in the public unraveling of the scandal was McQueary's testimony about what he observed in late February 2002. McQueary, then a 28-year-old graduate assistant, entered the Lasch Football Building shower facilities at Penn State after hours and witnessed Sandusky sexually abusing a boy he estimated to be approximately **10 years old**. McQueary left the building without intervening and called his father, who advised him to speak with Paterno the following morning.

Paterno received McQueary's account on a Saturday morning and telephoned Curley the following day to arrange a meeting. Paterno later testified that he relayed what McQueary had told him, though he described his understanding of the act as "inappropriate conduct" rather than rape. Curley and Schultz subsequently met with McQueary on **February 26**, heard his account, and decided to report the matter only to **The Second Mile** — stripping Sandusky of locker-room access but not contacting law enforcement or child welfare authorities.

Evidence later emerged that Penn State administrators had been aware of a **1998 investigation** in which campus police looked into Sandusky showering with a young boy and potentially abusive behavior. That earlier investigation was closed without charges. The Freeh Report concluded that the 2002 decision not to report was made with full knowledge of the 1998 incident.

## Arrest and Immediate Aftermath

The criminal investigation that led to Sandusky's November 2011 arrest had been ongoing for approximately **three years**, triggered by a 2008 complaint from a Clinton County, Pennsylvania school after a 15-year-old student reported abuse. Investigators gradually identified multiple victims, each of whom described patterns of grooming, access through The Second Mile, and abuse at Penn State facilities.

When the grand jury presentment was released publicly on **November 5, 2011**, it identified **eight victims** by number, detailed specific incidents of abuse, and named Paterno, Curley, and Schultz by role in the 2002 non-reporting. The public reaction was immediate and overwhelming: student protests erupted on Penn State's University Park campus, initially in defense of Paterno, with thousands gathering outside his home and in the downtown area.

On the night of **November 9**, the Board of Trustees convened and voted to terminate both Paterno and Spanier immediately. Paterno had attempted to manage the timeline by announcing earlier that day that he would retire at the end of the 2011 season — a statement the Board overrode by forcing his immediate termination. The firings triggered riots by a subset of students, who overturned a news van and clashed with police in scenes that drew widespread condemnation.

## The Freeh Report

Commissioned by the Penn State Board of Trustees and delivered on **July 12, 2012**, the **Freeh Report** — formally titled *Report of the Special Investigative Counsel Regarding the Actions of The Pennsylvania State University Related to the Child Sexual Abuse Committed by Gerald A. Sandusky* — was conducted by **Louis Freeh**, former Director of the FBI, and his law firm Freeh Sporkin & Sullivan.

The report's central finding was unequivocal: **"The most powerful men at Penn State failed to take any steps for 14 years to protect the children who Sandusky victimized."** Specifically, the report found that Paterno, Spanier, Curley, and Schultz had:

- Concealed Sandusky's abuse from authorities to avoid publicity and protect the football program's reputation
- Reversed a decision to report Sandusky to child welfare authorities in 2001 following a conversation with Paterno
- Failed to conduct any inquiry into Sandusky's conduct after 2002 despite ongoing access to university facilities

The **267-page report** drew on interviews with more than **430 people** and review of over **3.5 million documents** — including an email from Curley stating that after speaking with Paterno, he had changed his mind about reporting to the Pennsylvania Department of Public Welfare.

## Legal Outcomes

**Jerry Sandusky** was convicted on **June 22, 2012** on **45 of 48 counts** of child sexual abuse — the two counts on which he was acquitted were technical matters; all substantive charges were upheld. He was sentenced on **October 9, 2012** to **30 to 60 years in state prison**, effectively a life sentence for the then-68-year-old. Pennsylvania courts rejected his appeals.

**Graham Spanier** was convicted in 2017 on one count of **child endangerment** and sentenced to 4 to 12 months, two months of which were served in jail. **Tim Curley** and **Gary Schultz** each pleaded guilty to child endangerment charges in 2017 and received sentences of 7 to 23 months.

**Joe Paterno** died of **lung cancer on January 22, 2012** — six weeks after his dismissal and six months before the Freeh Report was released. He never faced criminal charges. The Freeh Report's criticism of him was disputed by his family, who commissioned their own independent investigation that reached different conclusions about what Paterno knew and when.

## NCAA Sanctions

On **July 23, 2012**, the **NCAA** imposed sanctions on Penn State's football program without conducting its own investigation — instead adopting the Freeh Report's findings. The sanctions were among the most severe in the organization's history:

- **$60 million fine**, directed to an endowment funding child welfare programs nationally
- **Four-year postseason ban** (2012–2015)
- Reduction of football scholarships from **85 to 65** for four years
- **Vacating of all wins from 1998 through 2011** — 111 victories, stripping Paterno of his record for wins among major college football coaches
- **Five-year probation**

The scholarship vacations and postseason ban were later reduced in 2014 following Penn State's cooperation with reform commitments. The vacated wins were restored in **January 2015** after the NCAA's enforcement committee reviewed the case.

**Joe Paterno's bronze statue**, which had stood outside Beaver Stadium since 2001, was removed by the university on **July 22, 2012** — one day before the NCAA sanctions were announced.

## Broader Impact

The Penn State scandal prompted lasting changes beyond the university:

- **Pennsylvania child-protection law reforms**: The Pennsylvania legislature passed sweeping amendments to the **Child Protective Services Law** in 2013, expanding mandatory reporting requirements and broadening the categories of adults required to report abuse to all adults working with children
- **National collegiate governance**: The NCAA's handling of sanctions without its own investigation — and the subsequent partial reversal — generated sustained criticism of the organization's disciplinary authority and processes
- **Institutional accountability frameworks**: Universities nationally reviewed their reporting structures for allegations against staff and updated their relationships with law enforcement following the scandal
- **The Second Mile** dissolved in December 2012, transferring its assets to another youth-serving organization following the reputational destruction caused by Sandusky's convictions

## Significance

The Penn State football scandal ranks among the most consequential **institutional failures in American sports history**. What made it singular was not only the severity of Sandusky's crimes — 45 criminal convictions spanning a decade and a half — but the documented evidence, confirmed in the Freeh Report, that multiple senior institutional leaders had **actively chosen to protect the institution over protecting children**. The scandal demonstrated how the prestige and financial power of a college football program could distort the judgment of university administrators to a catastrophic degree.

Paterno's fall from his status as a symbol of academic-athletic integrity — he had for decades been cited as proof that college football could operate honorably — was particularly jarring to the broader culture of collegiate sport. His dismissal, and the subsequent stripping of his wins, represented one of American sports' most complete reversals of legacy.

The scandal accelerated national conversations about mandatory reporting, institutional culture, and the dangers of placing athletic success above ethical accountability — conversations that continued to shape higher education policy throughout the 2010s.
