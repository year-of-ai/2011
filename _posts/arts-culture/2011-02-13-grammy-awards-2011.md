**Key figures**: Lady Antebellum, Arcade Fire, Eminem, Esperanza Spalding, Christopher Tin, LL Cool J (host)

## Summary

The 53rd Annual Grammy Awards ceremony took place on February 13, 2011, at the Staples Center in Los Angeles and was broadcast on CBS to 26.6 million viewers — the event's highest-rated broadcast in eight years. Hosted by LL Cool J, the three-and-a-half-hour ceremony celebrated achievement across the full spectrum of music genres and marked several historic firsts in Grammy history. Lady Antebellum dominated the awards with five total Grammy wins, while Arcade Fire made history as the first indie rock act to win Album of the Year. The ceremony also introduced the first Grammy ever awarded to a piece of music originally composed for a video game.

## Key Results

- **Album of the Year**: Arcade Fire, *The Suburbs* — the first indie act to win this category since its inception
- **Record of the Year**: Lady Antebellum, "Need You Now"
- **Song of the Year**: Lady Antebellum, "Need You Now" (Hillary Scott, Charles Kelley, Dave Haywood, Josh Kear, songwriters)
- **Best New Artist**: Esperanza Spalding, upsetting heavily favored Justin Bieber
- **Best Rap Album**: Eminem, *Recovery*
- **Best Pop Vocal Album**: Katy Perry, *Teenage Dream*
- **Best Rock Album**: Muse, *The Resistance*
- **Total Awards Presented**: 108 across all categories

## Notable Performances

The ceremony featured an unusually dense roster of live performances spanning genres. Eminem opened with a medley from *Recovery* joined by Dr. Dre and Rihanna. Lady Gaga delivered a theatrical performance accompanied by Elton John. Mick Jagger performed a tribute to recently deceased soul singer Solomon Burke with "Everybody Needs Somebody to Love." Barbra Streisand received the MusiCares Person of the Year award, and Aretha Franklin and Dolly Parton each received tribute segments highlighting their foundational influence on American music. Katy Perry, Bruno Mars, and Bob Dylan also performed during the broadcast.

## Historic Firsts

**Arcade Fire and indie recognition**: When Arcade Fire's *The Suburbs* (released August 2010, Merge Records) won Album of the Year, it became the first album on an independent label to win the Grammy's most prestigious award. The victory stunned many observers who had expected Lady Antebellum or Eminem to win; audience and social media reaction in real time was notably split, highlighting the gap between mainstream pop audiences and indie music listeners.

**Video game music Grammy**: Christopher Tin's choral composition "Baba Yetu" — originally written in 2005 for the strategy game *Civilization IV* — won Best Instrumental Arrangement Accompanying Vocalist(s). The piece is set in Swahili and uses the Lord's Prayer as its text. Its Grammy win, more than five years after its initial release in a game, was the first time the Recording Academy had recognized a work originating in a video game. This preceded *Skyrim*'s release by nearly nine months and reflected growing institutional recognition of games as a legitimate artistic medium. See also: [The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim](/news/arts-culture/skyrim-2011/).

**Esperanza Spalding upset**: The 25-year-old jazz bassist and vocalist was a relatively unknown figure to mainstream audiences when she won Best New Artist over Justin Bieber, Drake, Florence and the Machine, and Mumford & Sons. Her win generated significant controversy online and helped spark broader debates about Grammy relevance and genre diversity.

## Lady Antebellum's Sweep

Lady Antebellum won five awards on the night: Record of the Year, Song of the Year, Best Country Album, Best Country Song, and Best Country Performance by a Duo or Group with Vocals. The sweep reflected country music's powerful crossover into mainstream pop audiences in the late 2000s and early 2010s. "Need You Now" had already spent five weeks at number one on the Billboard Hot Country Songs chart and crossed over to the mainstream pop charts, demonstrating country music's expanding commercial reach.

## Context in the 2011 Music Year

The 53rd Grammy ceremony fell in the middle of a particularly strong year for recorded music. Adele's *21*, released in January 2011, would go on to dominate the following year's Grammys (54th), winning six awards including Album and Record of the Year. See also: [Adele's *21* Album](/news/arts-culture/adele-21-album/). Amy Winehouse, who had previously won five Grammy Awards at the 50th ceremony in 2008, died in July 2011 without further recording. See also: [Amy Winehouse](/news/people/amy-winehouse/).

The 83rd Academy Awards took place two weeks later, on February 27, 2011, creating a concentrated awards-season window in February. See also: [83rd Academy Awards](/news/arts-culture/academy-awards-2011/).

## Significance

The 2011 Grammy Awards represented a pivotal moment in the music industry's relationship with indie and alternative rock. Arcade Fire's triumph over established major-label acts like Eminem and Lady Antebellum signified growing acceptance of independently distributed music at the industry's highest recognition level, validating a movement that had built momentum through the 2000s on the strength of blogs, streaming, and word-of-mouth rather than traditional radio promotion. Christopher Tin's historic Grammy for video game music acknowledged a medium that had long been overlooked by the Recording Academy despite reaching hundreds of millions of listeners worldwide. Esperanza Spalding's Best New Artist award underscored the Grammys' capacity to recognize jazz and cross-genre innovation — and generated the kind of mainstream controversy that only a major surprise can produce.
