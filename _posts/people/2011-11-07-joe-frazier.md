## Summary

Joseph William Frazier, known as "Smokin' Joe," died on November 7, 2011, at his home in Philadelphia at age 67, weeks after being diagnosed with liver cancer. Born January 12, 1944, in Beaufort, South Carolina, Frazier won the heavyweight gold medal at the 1964 Tokyo Olympics as a late replacement for an injured teammate, then turned professional and became undisputed heavyweight champion of the world in 1970. He is best remembered for his trilogy of fights against Muhammad Ali — including the 1971 "Fight of the Century," in which he became the first man to defeat Ali as a professional — and for his two title losses to George Foreman.

Frazier's aggressive, relentless fighting style, built around a devastating left hook, made him one of the most feared heavyweights of boxing's golden era in the 1970s. He retired from professional competition in 1976 (with a brief, unsuccessful 1981 comeback), finishing with a record of 32 wins, 4 losses, and 1 draw, 27 by knockout, and went on to train fighters, including his son Marvis, at his Philadelphia gym.

## Early Life

Frazier was born January 12, 1944, in Beaufort, South Carolina, the seventh of twelve children of Rubin and Dolly Frazier, sharecroppers who farmed on Hilton Head Island. He left home for New York City around 1959 and then settled in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, where he found work in a slaughterhouse. He took up boxing at a local gym and was trained initially by Yank Durham, a former boxer who became Frazier's manager and primary trainer through the height of his career. Durham's rigorous conditioning approach — emphasizing relentless pressure, body work, and a crouching, bobbing style that made Frazier a difficult target — shaped the fighter's entire professional identity.

## Olympic Career and Early Professional Record

Frazier qualified for the 1964 Tokyo Olympics in the heavyweight division as a replacement for Buster Mathis, who suffered a broken knuckle. Despite breaking his left thumb during the Olympic tournament's semifinal bout — an injury that significantly hampered his power in that fight — Frazier won the gold medal by defeating Hans Huber of West Germany in the final. His amateur record and Olympic title formed the foundation for his professional debut in 1965.

Frazier turned professional under Durham's management in August 1965 and compiled an undefeated record through the late 1960s, developing one of the most formidable left hooks in heavyweight history. He won the New York State Athletic Commission heavyweight title in March 1968 by defeating Buster Mathis (the same Mathis who had been injured at the Olympic trials) and unified the heavyweight championship on February 16, 1970, by knocking out Jimmy Ellis in five rounds at Madison Square Garden, becoming recognized as the undisputed world heavyweight champion.

## The Ali Trilogy

Frazier and Muhammad Ali fought three times between 1971 and 1975, a rivalry regarded as one of the greatest in boxing history. Both the political context — Ali's draft refusal and Frazier's more politically conventional public image — and the quality of the fights gave the rivalry a cultural weight beyond sport:

- **March 8, 1971 — "The Fight of the Century"** at Madison Square Garden, New York. The contest was one of the most anticipated in boxing history: both men entered the ring undefeated, with Ali holding a 31–0 record and Frazier 26–0. The fight drew a closed-circuit television audience estimated at 300 million viewers worldwide and generated a live gate of approximately $1.35 million. Frazier won by unanimous decision over 15 rounds, knocking Ali down with a left hook in the 15th, handing the previously unbeaten Ali his first professional loss and retaining his heavyweight title.
- **January 28, 1974 — Non-title rematch** at Madison Square Garden. Ali won by unanimous decision over 12 rounds. Neither man's heavyweight title was at stake.
- **October 1, 1975 — "Thrilla in Manila"** at the Araneta Coliseum in Quezon City, Philippines, for Ali's WBC and WBA heavyweight titles. Widely considered one of the most brutal heavyweight championship fights in history, the bout was contested in temperatures exceeding 100 degrees Fahrenheit. Ali's trainer Angelo Dundee considered stopping the fight after the 14th round due to Ali's exhaustion; instead, Eddie Futch — Frazier's chief second, who had replaced Durham (who died in 1973) — refused to allow Frazier to continue after the 14th round, citing swelling that had nearly shut both of Frazier's eyes. Ali was declared the winner by technical knockout.

Frazier lost his title to George Foreman on January 22, 1973, in Kingston, Jamaica, when Foreman knocked him down six times before referee Arthur Mercante stopped the fight in the second round. A rematch with Foreman on June 15, 1976, which Foreman also won by fifth-round knockout, effectively ended Frazier's tenure as a championship contender.

## Post-Retirement Life

Frazier retired after the Foreman rematch in 1976, though he attempted a brief comeback in December 1981, losing to Floyd Cummings in a draw. He operated Joe Frazier's Gym in North Philadelphia for many years, training amateur and professional fighters including his son Marvis Frazier, who reached the heavyweight championship level in the early 1980s. Frazier was a fixture in Philadelphia civic life and maintained a complicated public relationship with Ali, whose repeated taunts and characterizations — particularly calling Frazier a "gorilla" during the Manila pre-fight promotion — remained a source of lasting resentment that Frazier discussed in interviews throughout his later life.

## Death and Tributes

Frazier was diagnosed with liver cancer in early October 2011 and entered home hospice care in Philadelphia. He died on the evening of November 7, 2011. Tributes came from across the sports world, including from Ali himself, who called Frazier "a great champion and one of the greatest heavyweights of all time." Frazier was inducted into the International Boxing Hall of Fame as part of its inaugural class in 1990. Frazier died in the same calendar year as other major figures including [Steve Jobs](/news/people/steve-jobs/) (October 5) and [Elizabeth Taylor](/news/people/elizabeth-taylor/) (March 23).

## Significance

Frazier's death closed out one of boxing's defining eras. His three fights against Muhammad Ali are widely regarded among the greatest in the sport's history and helped define heavyweight boxing's peak popularity in the 1970s. His 1971 win over Ali remains a landmark — the first loss of Ali's professional career — and "Thrilla in Manila" is frequently cited by historians and fighters alike as among the most grueling championship contests ever contested. Frazier's death in 2011, with Ali already declining from Parkinson's disease, prompted renewed public reflection on the physical toll of the sport and the complexities of their long rivalry, which had simultaneously elevated both men to global fame while leaving each with scars the other had inflicted.
