## Summary

Throughout 2011, a cascade of revelations dismantled one of Britain's most-read Sunday newspapers and triggered the most consequential crisis in British press history. The *News of the World*, founded in 1843 and with a circulation exceeding 2.6 million copies per week, was exposed as having systematically authorized private investigators to hack into the voicemail messages of celebrities, politicians, crime victims, and ordinary members of the public.

Initial hacking allegations had surfaced in 2006 when royal editor Clive Goodman and private investigator Glenn Mulcaire were arrested; both were jailed in January 2007. News International, the Murdoch subsidiary that owned the paper, insisted the practice was confined to a single "rogue reporter." That containment narrative collapsed in 2011.

### The Milly Dowler Revelation

The pivotal moment came on July 4, 2011, when *The Guardian* reported that the *News of the World* had intercepted voicemail messages on the mobile phone of Milly Dowler, a 13-year-old schoolgirl who was abducted and murdered in 2002. The initial report also stated that messages had been deleted while she was still missing, giving her family false hope that she was alive; *The Guardian* subsequently acknowledged, in December 2011, that the deletions could not be reliably attributed to the newspaper and may have resulted from the phone system's automatic purging of old messages. The core revelation that Dowler's phone had been hacked nonetheless generated unprecedented public outrage and transformed the scandal from a political embarrassment into a national crisis.

Within days, further reports emerged that the newspaper had hacked the phones of the families of soldiers killed in Afghanistan and Iraq, victims of the July 7, 2005, London bombings, and relatives of victims of the Soham murders.

### Closure of *News of the World*

On July 7, 2011 — exactly three days after the Milly Dowler story broke — News Corporation announced that *News of the World* would cease publication after its issue of July 10, 2011. The final edition, carrying the headline "Thank You & Goodbye," was published on July 10 and sold approximately 4.5 million copies. It was the newspaper's 8,674th and final edition, ending 168 years of continuous publication. The closure cost around 200 journalists their jobs, most of whom had no involvement in the hacking.

The same week, News Corporation withdrew its £7.8 billion bid to take full control of British Sky Broadcasting (BSkyB), recognizing that the political environment made regulatory approval impossible.

### Parliamentary and Legal Proceedings

On July 19, 2011, Rupert Murdoch and his son James Murdoch appeared before the House of Commons Culture, Media and Sport Select Committee — a rare spectacle of the world's most powerful media mogul testifying under parliamentary scrutiny. During the session, a protestor attempted to hit Rupert Murdoch with a shaving-foam pie; Murdoch's then-wife Wendi Deng deflected the attack. Murdoch told MPs it was "the most humble day of my life."

Rebekah Brooks was arrested on July 17, 2011. By September 2011 police had arrested over a dozen current and former *News of the World* employees. Andy Coulson, who had served as Prime Minister David Cameron's communications director from 2010 until January 2011, was arrested on July 8. The proximity between Coulson and Downing Street drew Cameron directly into the scandal and raised questions about judgment at the highest levels of the British government.

### The Leveson Inquiry

Prime Minister David Cameron announced on July 13, 2011, that Lord Justice Brian Leveson would chair a public inquiry into press culture, ethics, and practices. The **Leveson Inquiry** began hearings in November 2011 and called over 300 witnesses, including politicians, celebrities, journalists, and victims. It published its 2,000-page report in November 2012, recommending a new independent regulatory body backed by statute — a recommendation that sparked a political battle between press freedom advocates and privacy campaigners that persisted for years.

## Significance

The *News of the World* phone hacking scandal of 2011 was a defining institutional rupture of the year. The closure of a 168-year-old newspaper with a circulation of 2.6 million demonstrated that even the most commercially powerful media organisations could be destroyed by documented ethical failures. It exposed structural corruption linking Fleet Street, the Metropolitan Police (officers accepted payments from the *News of the World* in exchange for tips), and the political establishment — a nexus that had been mutually protective for decades.

The scandal formed part of a broader 2011 confrontation with entrenched institutional power, sitting alongside events like the [Arab Spring]({{ '/news/history-politics/arab-spring/' | relative_url }}) and the [WikiLeaks / Bradley Manning disclosures]({{ '/news/history-politics/wikileaks-bradley-manning/' | relative_url }}) as moments when citizens and investigators stripped away concealment maintained by powerful actors. The Leveson Inquiry became a proxy war over the meaning of press freedom versus press accountability that outlasted the inquiry itself.

Internationally, the scandal prompted scrutiny of News Corporation's US operations, including *Fox News* and the *Wall Street Journal*, and contributed to the company's decision in June 2013 to split into two separately listed entities: News Corp (print) and 21st Century Fox (entertainment). The episode established a lasting template for how corporate crisis management, if built on false denials, can accelerate institutional collapse.
