## Background

Caylee Marie Anthony was reported missing on July 15, 2008 — 31 days after her mother, Casey Anthony, claimed she had last seen her. Casey, then 22 years old, had told her parents that Caylee was being cared for by a babysitter named "Zenaida Fernandez-Gonzalez," a person investigators could not locate and who, it later emerged, did not exist. Caylee had last been seen alive on June 16, 2008, the same day Casey and Caylee were last observed together on home video at Casey's parents' house.

During the 31 days Caylee was missing, Casey had photographed herself partying at a nightclub in Orlando, received a tattoo reading *bella vita* ("beautiful life" in Italian), shoplifted from Target, and told elaborate stories to her family and friends. When her mother, Cindy Anthony, finally located her and demanded to know Caylee's whereabouts, she reported the child missing to authorities.

On December 11, 2008, Caylee's skeletal remains were found in a wooded area approximately 15 houses from the Anthony family home in Orlando, Florida. A heart-shaped sticker residue was found on duct tape discovered near the skull, and the remains were in a garbage bag. The medical examiner ruled the death a homicide but could not determine the cause of death with certainty, given the decomposition of the remains.

Casey Anthony was arrested on October 14, 2008 on charges of first-degree murder, aggravated child abuse, aggravated manslaughter, and four counts of providing false information to law enforcement.

## The Trial

### Proceedings (May 24 – July 5, 2011)

Opening statements began May 24, 2011, before Judge Belvin Perry Jr. in Orlando's Orange County Courthouse. The trial lasted approximately six weeks and featured testimony from 90 witnesses.

**The prosecution's theory**: State Attorney Jeff Ashton argued that Casey had killed Caylee by administering chloroform and then applying duct tape over her mouth and nose to suffocate her, in order to free herself from parenting responsibilities. Key evidence included:

- Computer searches for "how to make chloroform" conducted 84 times on the family computer in March 2008, according to forensic investigators (later disputed; the number was revised to a single search, a discrepancy that became significant during closing arguments)
- High levels of chloroform allegedly detected in the trunk of Casey's car by an FBI laboratory — a finding challenged by the defense
- Testimony from Caylee's grandfather, George Anthony, about the timeline of Casey and Caylee's last observed departure
- Photographs of Casey partying at a nightclub, including a "hot body contest," taken during the 31 days Caylee was missing
- The duct tape found near Caylee's remains, which prosecutors claimed matched duct tape found in the Anthony home

**The defense's theory**: José Baez argued that Caylee had accidentally drowned in the family's backyard swimming pool on June 16, 2008, and that Casey's father, George Anthony, had discovered the body and helped cover it up rather than report it. Baez suggested Casey's behavior — the lies, the partying — was a coping mechanism she had developed as the result of years of sexual abuse by her father, abuse George Anthony denied. The defense argued no evidence of a murder weapon, no proven cause of death, and no meaningful motive had been established beyond speculation.

### Jury Deliberations and Verdict

Closing arguments concluded July 3, 2011. After approximately 10 hours and 40 minutes of deliberation — remarkably brief for a capital murder case — the jury of seven women and five men returned on July 5, 2011, with its verdict:

- **Not guilty**: first-degree murder
- **Not guilty**: aggravated manslaughter
- **Not guilty**: aggravated child abuse
- **Guilty**: four counts of providing false information to law enforcement (misdemeanors)

Judge Perry sentenced Casey Anthony to four years — one year per count — but credited her with time already served (approximately 1,043 days). She was released from Orange County Jail on July 17, 2011, just 12 days after the verdict.

## Significance

### Public Reaction and the "Trial of the Century" Label

The verdict triggered an immediate national backlash. Outside the Orange County Courthouse, crowds gathered to protest. Social media — Twitter in particular — was flooded with condemnations of the jury and, in some cases, of José Baez and Judge Perry. In the days that followed, legal analysts noted a stark divergence between public opinion (which largely presumed Casey's guilt) and the jury's legal conclusion (that the state had not proven guilt beyond a reasonable doubt). Opinion polls conducted in July 2011 showed that 64–75% of Americans believed Casey Anthony was guilty.

The public furor illuminated a tension inherent in Anglo-American criminal justice: "beyond a reasonable doubt" is a deliberately high evidentiary bar, one that does not require proof of what actually happened — only proof sufficient to exclude reasonable alternative explanations. Jurors who spoke to the media after the verdict described feeling that the evidence was circumstantial and insufficient. The lead prosecutor, Jeff Ashton, later wrote that he believed Casey was guilty and that the trial had been lost partly due to the failure of the chloroform search count and the unpredictable nature of jury deliberation.

### Media Coverage and the True Crime Industrial Complex

The Casey Anthony trial coincided with the early maturation of cable-news saturation coverage of criminal trials. HLN (formerly Headline News) provided gavel-to-gavel coverage and built its primetime programming — anchored by Nancy Grace — almost entirely around the case during the trial period. Grace, who had called Casey "tot mom" throughout her coverage and maintained near-constant presumption of guilt, drew 1.3 million nightly viewers at the trial's peak.

The case's relationship to media was not merely parasitic: media attention shaped public understanding and arguably heightened the stakes. The Occupy Wall Street movement that erupted that September (see [Occupy Wall Street](/news/society-economics/occupy-wall-street/)) would also be shaped by social-media amplification, but the Anthony trial demonstrated how legacy cable and social media could fuse into a single outrage feedback loop — a pattern that would define high-profile events for the rest of the decade.

The trial's legacy is most visible in the true crime documentary and podcast boom of the 2010s: *Serial* (2014), *Making a Murderer* (2015), and dozens of subsequent productions that treated wrongful-conviction or controversial-verdict cases as cultural events. The Anthony verdict gave millions of Americans their first sustained experience of following a trial as an ongoing episodic narrative.

### Parallel Media Scandals of July 2011

The Casey Anthony verdict fell in the same news cycle as the [News of the World phone-hacking scandal](/news/history-politics/news-of-the-world-scandal-2011/), which broke publicly in early July 2011 and led to the closure of the UK's highest-circulation Sunday newspaper. Both events, occurring within days of each other, placed media ethics — the boundaries of press conduct, the responsibilities of coverage, and the relationship between public sentiment and factual accuracy — at the center of public discourse on both sides of the Atlantic.

### Aftermath

After her release, Casey Anthony lived in secrecy, reportedly moving through a series of locations in Florida to avoid public recognition. She pursued civil litigation for several years, suing Zenaida Fernandez-Gonzalez (the real person whose name she had used as an alibi) for defamation, and defending herself against a defamation suit filed by the private investigator she had hired. In 2022, she participated in a documentary series, *Casey Anthony: Where the Truth Lies*, for Peacock, in which she maintained her innocence while providing her first extended on-camera account of Caylee's death — claiming her father had indeed discovered the body.

The legal case remains officially unsolved: no one has been convicted of Caylee Anthony's death.
