## Summary

Instagram, the photo-sharing and social-networking application, launched on the Apple App Store on October 6, 2010, as an iOS-only platform. The app was built by Kevin Systrom and Mike Krieger, who had spent approximately eight weeks stripping an earlier check-in app called Burbn down to its photographic core. Instagram reached 25,000 registered users on its first day and 100,000 users within its first week — one of the fastest user-acquisition trajectories in App Store history to that point. By December 2010, Instagram had surpassed one million registered users, achieved without a single dollar of paid marketing.

By the end of 2011 — a year of rapid smartphone adoption driven by the [iPhone 4S and Siri launch]({{ '/news/science-technology/iphone-4s-siri/' | relative_url }}) and the rise of the [Android ecosystem]({{ '/news/science-technology/android-mobile-rise/' | relative_url }}) — Instagram had grown to over ten million registered users on iOS alone and raised $7 million in Series A funding (February 2011) followed by a $50 million Series B in April 2012, which closed simultaneously with Facebook's acquisition announcement.

## From Burbn to Instagram

Kevin Systrom, a Stanford University graduate with a background in design and an early internship at Odeo (the podcast company that pivoted to become Twitter), began building Burbn in late 2009. The app was a location-based check-in service similar to Foursquare, with a photo-sharing component. Systrom raised a seed round of $500,000 from Baseline Ventures and Andreessen Horowitz in early 2010.

After Systrom partnered with Mike Krieger — a Stanford HCI (human-computer interaction) graduate and UX designer — the two concluded that Burbn was too complicated. They stripped everything from the app except its photo-sharing, liking, and commenting features, redesigning around three core principles:

1. **Speed**: photos had to upload and appear in a follower's feed faster than any competing app.
2. **Simplicity**: the interface would have one primary action — posting a photo — with minimal secondary menus.
3. **Aesthetics**: every photo would be square, echoing the Kodak Instamatic camera format, creating visual consistency across feeds.

The name "Instagram" combined "instant" (from instant cameras) with "telegram" (signifying rapid, direct communication).

## Launch Metrics and Growth Trajectory

| Date | Milestone |
|---|---|
| October 6, 2010 | App Store launch (iOS only); 25,000 users day one |
| October 12, 2010 | 100,000 registered users |
| December 2010 | 1 million registered users |
| February 2011 | $7 million Series A (Benchmark Capital) |
| July 2011 | 5 million registered users |
| September 2011 | 10 million registered users |
| April 3, 2012 | Android version launched; 1 million new users in 12 hours |
| April 9, 2012 | Facebook acquisition announced for $1 billion (cash and stock) |

The growth curve was notable for being driven almost entirely by word-of-mouth and the network effect of existing users sharing their Instagram photos to Twitter and Facebook. During 2011, Instagram photos shared to Twitter carried a link back to Instagram's mobile web preview, creating a viral loop that exposed non-users to the aesthetic and encouraged app downloads.

## Filters, Design Philosophy, and the Square Format

Instagram launched with eleven filters — including Toaster, Walden, Valencia, Sierra, and X-Pro II — each inspired by analog photographic processes: overexposure, cross-processing, expired film, and Polaroid color shifts. The filters served a practical and psychological purpose: they transformed ordinary, mediocre smartphone photos into images that looked considered and artistic.

The square format (1:1 aspect ratio) was a deliberate departure from the 4:3 rectangular format of smartphone cameras. Systrom has cited the Kodak Instamatic and the Polaroid Land Camera as inspirations. Squareness had two advantages: it fit the standard smartphone screen width without letterboxing, and it created a uniform grid aesthetic in user profiles and feeds that rectangular photos would have disrupted.

The app's stripped-down interface — a home feed, a search tab, a camera button, a notifications tab, and a profile tab — embodied the "less is more" design philosophy of the era. The design stood in contrast to Facebook's feature density and even to [Google+]({{ '/news/science-technology/google-plus-launch-2011/' | relative_url }}), which launched in June 2011 with a richer but more complex feature set.

## Significance

Instagram's 2010–2011 growth arc represented a decisive inflection in mobile social media. Several forces converged:

- **Camera quality**: the iPhone 4 (launched June 2010) featured a significantly improved 5-megapixel camera, making smartphone photography aesthetically viable for the first time for a broad consumer base.
- **App Store maturity**: by late 2010, the App Store had been open for two years and had a large installed base of iOS users accustomed to discovering and installing apps rapidly.
- **Social graph portability**: Instagram allowed users to connect their Twitter and Facebook accounts, bootstrapping their social graph without starting from zero.

The app's rise posed an existential challenge to Flickr, Yahoo's photo-sharing platform, which had dominated online photography sharing since 2004 but had grown slowly in the mobile era. Flickr's registered user base stagnated through 2011 as Instagram's surged; this shift marked the moment when mobile-native apps definitively displaced desktop-web incumbents in consumer photo sharing.

The $1 billion Facebook acquisition in April 2012, closing on the same day as Instagram's Android launch, was the largest acquisition of a venture-backed startup with fewer than 20 employees in Silicon Valley history at the time. Instagram had 13 employees at acquisition. The deal reflected Mark Zuckerberg's recognition that Instagram's visual, mobile-first model represented the future of social sharing at a moment when Facebook itself was under pressure over its mobile experience. [Steve Jobs]({{ '/news/people/steve-jobs/' | relative_url }}) died on October 5, 2011 — the day before Instagram's first anniversary — and his death accelerated attention to Apple's mobile ecosystem and the apps that defined it.
