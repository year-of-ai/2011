# Death of Wangari Maathai

**Category**: People  
**Key figures**: Wangari Muta Maathai (April 1, 1940 – September 26, 2011)

## Summary

Wangari Maathai, the Kenyan environmental and political activist who became the first African woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize, died on September 26, 2011, at Nairobi Hospital, Kenya, from complications of ovarian cancer. She was 71 years old. Her death removed one of the most internationally recognised voices at the intersection of environmental conservation, democratic rights, and gender equality.

### Early Life and Education

Born on April 1, 1940, in Ihithe village in the Nyeri District of central Kenya (then British Kenya), Maathai was one of the first women in East and Central Africa to earn a doctorate. She studied biological sciences at Mount St. Scholastica College in Atchison, Kansas (1964), earned a master's degree from the University of Pittsburgh (1966), and received her Ph.D. from the University of Nairobi in 1971 — the first woman to do so in that institution's history. She became the first woman in East and Central Africa to earn a doctoral degree and the first female professor at the University of Nairobi.

### The Green Belt Movement

In 1977, Maathai founded the **Green Belt Movement** under the auspices of the National Council of Women of Kenya. The organisation grew out of a recognition that rural women were suffering from shortages of firewood, clean water, and food because of widespread deforestation. Rather than seeking government action, the movement mobilised women at the grassroots level to plant trees on farms, schools, and church compounds.

By the time of Maathai's death in 2011, the Green Belt Movement had planted **over 51 million trees** across Kenya and had exported its model to more than 30 African countries. The movement employed or mobilised an estimated 900,000 women and had established 6,000 nurseries across Kenya. It became a model for integrating environmental conservation with women's empowerment and community development — a template subsequently cited by the United Nations Environment Programme and global development organisations.

### Political Career and Imprisonment

Maathai's activism brought her into direct conflict with the authoritarian government of President Daniel arap Moi. She was arrested and imprisoned multiple times through the 1990s. In 1992, she led protests against political detainees that resulted in her arrest; the protest camp in Uhuru Park, Nairobi, became internationally recognised. In 1999, she physically lay down in front of bulldozers attempting to raze Karura Forest in Nairobi for a government-connected housing development — an act of defiance that drew global media coverage and ultimately saved the forest.

She won a seat in Kenya's parliament in the December 2002 elections with 98 percent of the vote in the Tetu constituency, Nyeri District. She served as Assistant Minister for Environment, Natural Resources, and Wildlife from January 2003 to November 2005 under President Mwai Kibaki's government.

### Nobel Peace Prize (2004)

On October 8, 2004, the Norwegian Nobel Committee awarded Maathai the Nobel Peace Prize "for her contribution to sustainable development, democracy and peace." She was the first African woman to receive the Nobel Peace Prize. The committee stated: "Peace on earth depends on our ability to secure our living environment." Maathai received the prize on December 10, 2004, in Oslo.

The award was notable for explicitly linking environmental conservation to peace and governance — a framing that expanded the committee's traditional focus on conflict resolution. Maathai used the platform to speak internationally on climate change, deforestation, and the connection between environmental degradation and poverty, violence, and political instability in Africa.

### Final Years

After leaving parliament in 2007, Maathai remained active internationally. She served as a UN Messenger of Peace with a special focus on environment and climate change from 2009 to 2011. She published four books, including her memoir *Unbowed* (2006), which became a bestseller in multiple countries. In 2010, she co-chaired the Copenhagen Climate Change Conference delegation for African civil society groups.

She was diagnosed with ovarian cancer and underwent treatment in 2011. She died on September 26, 2011, at Nairobi Hospital. In accordance with her wishes, she was not embalmed, and her body was wrapped in a hyacinth mat — a non-timber product — rather than a wooden coffin, underscoring her environmental commitments even in death. She was buried at the Karura Forest in Nairobi, the same forest she had fought to protect in 1999.

## Significance

Maathai's death in 2011 closed a chapter in African environmental activism that was simultaneously political, feminist, and ecological. Her dual legacy was as a practitioner — the Green Belt Movement's 51 million trees represented a measurable, scalable intervention against desertification and climate vulnerability — and as a symbol: proof that grassroots environmental organising could challenge both ecological destruction and authoritarian governance.

Her work gained additional resonance in 2011 against the backdrop of the [East Africa drought and famine](../society-economics/east-africa-drought-2011.md), which devastated Kenya, Somalia, and Ethiopia in the same year. The crisis underlined precisely the food and water insecurity that the Green Belt Movement had spent three decades combating.

Maathai joined a striking number of prominent figures lost in 2011, including [Steve Jobs](steve-jobs.md), [Amy Winehouse](amy-winehouse.md), [Christopher Hitchens](christopher-hitchens.md), [Dennis Ritchie](dennis-ritchie.md), and [Václav Havel](../history-politics/vaclav-havel-death.md) — a year whose losses spanned technological, cultural, and political domains. Among these, Maathai stood apart as the figure whose work was most directly addressed to long-term planetary conditions rather than immediate events.

Her influence endured institutionally: the Green Belt Movement continued its work under new leadership; Kenya's 2010 constitution, which she had championed, included environmental rights provisions; and her model was cited by the African Union's Great Green Wall initiative, which aims to plant an 8,000-kilometre belt of trees across the Sahel.
