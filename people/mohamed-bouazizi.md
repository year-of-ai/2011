# Death of Mohamed Bouazizi

**Category**: People  
**Key figures**: Mohamed Bouazizi (March 29, 1984 – January 4, 2011)

## Summary

Mohamed Bouazizi was a Tunisian street vendor whose act of self-immolation on December 17, 2010, in the provincial town of Sidi Bouzid became the catalyst for the Tunisian Revolution and the wider Arab Spring. Set alight in protest at official harassment and the confiscation of his produce, he suffered burns over roughly 90 percent of his body and died on January 4, 2011, at the Ben Arous burn centre near Tunis. His death, at the age of 26, transformed a local grievance into a national uprising and made him a posthumous symbol of dignity and resistance across the Arab world.

## Background

Bouazizi was born on March 29, 1984, in Sidi Bouzid, an impoverished agricultural town in central Tunisia. After his father died when he was young, he became a principal breadwinner for his mother, stepfather, and siblings. Unable to find formal employment, he sold fruit and vegetables from a cart, reportedly earning around US$140 a month while supporting several relatives and helping to fund a sister's university education.

Street vendors in Tunisia routinely operated without permits and were subject to fines, bribes, and the confiscation of their goods by municipal officials — a persistent source of friction in a country where youth unemployment was high and public frustration with corruption and the authoritarian rule of President Zine El Abidine Ben Ali had been mounting for years.

## The Self-Immolation

On the morning of December 17, 2010, municipal officials confronted Bouazizi and confiscated his produce and electronic weighing scales, reportedly because he lacked a vendor's permit. Accounts widely circulated at the time held that a municipal official, Faida Hamdi, slapped or otherwise humiliated him during the confrontation — a claim Hamdi later disputed and for which she was subsequently cleared in court. Bouazizi went to the provincial governor's office to demand the return of his scales and to lodge a complaint, but officials refused to see him.

Within roughly an hour of being turned away, Bouazizi returned to the front of the governor's building, doused himself with a flammable liquid, and set himself on fire in public. He was hospitalised with catastrophic burns.

## Death and the Spark of Revolution

News and images of the self-immolation spread rapidly through Sidi Bouzid and, via social media and satellite broadcasters such as Al Jazeera, across Tunisia. Protests erupted in Sidi Bouzid within hours and spread to other towns over the following weeks, met by a violent police response. On December 28, 2010, President Ben Ali visited Bouazizi in hospital in a widely publicised attempt to defuse the unrest.

Bouazizi died on January 4, 2011. His funeral drew a large crowd, and the demonstrations intensified into a nationwide movement demanding the end of Ben Ali's rule. On January 14, 2011 — ten days after Bouazizi's death — Ben Ali fled to Saudi Arabia, ending 23 years in power. The uprising, later known as the Jasmine Revolution, became the first successful revolution of the Arab Spring.

## Significance

Bouazizi's death is widely regarded as the immediate trigger of the [Tunisian Revolution](../history-politics/tunisian-revolution-2011.md) and, through it, of the broader [Arab Spring](../history-politics/arab-spring.md) — the wave of uprisings that spread in 2011 to Egypt, Libya, Yemen, Syria, Bahrain, and beyond. His story, that of an ordinary vendor humiliated by a corrupt bureaucracy, resonated across societies where similar grievances over dignity, unemployment, and repression were widespread.

He became an international symbol posthumously. Tunisia's post-revolution government renamed a Sidi Bouzid square and a central Tunis avenue in his honour, issued a commemorative postage stamp, and cited him as the emblem of the revolution. In 2011 he was named, collectively with "the Arab Spring protester," among *Time* magazine's runners-up recognitions of the year's defining figures, and *The Times* of London named him its Person of the Year. His name recurs in analyses of how a single act can crystallise long-accumulating social tension into political change.

The wider legacy proved complex: while Tunisia achieved a durable democratic transition — later recognised by the 2015 Nobel Peace Prize awarded to the Tunisian National Dialogue Quartet — several other Arab Spring uprisings gave way to civil war or renewed authoritarian rule. Bouazizi remained the figure most consistently invoked as the spark of the entire sequence of events.
