# Television in 2011

**Category**: Arts & Culture  
**Key figures**: David Benioff, D.B. Weiss, Vince Gilligan, Matthew Weiner, Ryan Murphy, Jessica Lange, Bryan Cranston, Aaron Paul

## Summary

Television in 2011 marked a watershed moment in the medium's evolution, characterized by the rise of prestige television as a genuine alternative to cinema, the continued fragmentation of viewership across cable and emerging digital platforms, and a decisive shift toward serialized storytelling that rewarded dedicated audiences. While broadcast television remained the dominant medium by raw viewership numbers, cable networks — HBO, AMC, Showtime, FX — established themselves as the primary incubators of ambitious, adult-oriented drama that rivaled film in artistic ambition and cultural impact.

The year's most consequential debut was *Game of Thrones* (HBO, April 17, 2011), which opened to 2.2 million viewers for its pilot and would average 2.5 million live viewers across Season 1 — modest by broadcast standards but massive for a premium cable debut. Breaking records for a fantasy production, the show earned 13 Emmy nominations in its first season and quickly became HBO's most-discussed property since *The Sopranos*. Alongside it, *Breaking Bad* Season 4 (AMC, July–October 2011) produced what many critics would later call the finest single season in television history, and *American Horror Story* (FX, October 5, 2011) pioneered the seasonal anthology format.

Internationally, the UK's *Downton Abbey* (which premiered in the U.S. on PBS in January 2011) became a mainstream phenomenon among American audiences, while Danish crime drama *The Killing* (*Forbrydelsen*) introduced North Americans to Scandinavian noir. Netflix, still primarily a DVD and licensed-streaming service with approximately 24 million U.S. subscribers at year's start, was quietly building the infrastructure for original content production that would arrive with *House of Cards* in 2013.

## Key Premieres and Major Events

### Game of Thrones (HBO, April 17, 2011)

Adapted by David Benioff and D.B. Weiss from George R. R. Martin's *A Song of Ice and Fire* novels, *Game of Thrones* premiered to 2.2 million viewers — strong enough for HBO to green-light Season 2 within two days of the pilot airing. The show's combination of political intrigue, sweeping medieval world-building, and willingness to kill major characters (including its apparent protagonist, Eddard Stark, in episode 9) immediately differentiated it from prior fantasy television. The season-one finale drew 3 million viewers, a 36% increase over the premiere, demonstrating strong word-of-mouth momentum.

The production budget of approximately $60 million for the first season — roughly $6 million per episode — was unprecedented for television fantasy. Shot on location in Northern Ireland, Malta, and Morocco, the series brought cinematic production values to the small screen in a way that television had rarely attempted. It earned 13 Emmy nominations for Season 1 and won two (Outstanding Supporting Actor for Peter Dinklage; Outstanding Main Title Design), beginning an awards dominance that would continue for years. See also: [Game of Thrones Premiere](game-of-thrones-premiere-2011.md).

### Breaking Bad, Season 4 (AMC, July 17 – October 9, 2011)

Season 4 of *Breaking Bad* is widely regarded as the series' creative peak and among the finest seasons of television ever produced. The 13-episode season documented Walter White's complete moral transformation from a man rationalizing his drug-manufacturing as self-protection into a calculating, ruthless criminal. The season finale, "Face Off" (October 9, 2011), delivered a shocking resolution to the season-long cat-and-mouse between Walter and cartel kingpin Gustavo Fring, drawing the series' highest ratings to that point — 1.9 million viewers — and setting up the final season.

*Breaking Bad* Season 4 earned Bryan Cranston his third consecutive Emmy for Outstanding Lead Actor in a Drama Series (he had won for Seasons 2 and 3 as well), and Aaron Paul won his second Emmy for Outstanding Supporting Actor. The season's critical reception placed it alongside the finest television of any era.

### American Horror Story (FX, October 5, 2011)

Ryan Murphy's anthology horror series premiered with "Murder House," anchored by Jessica Lange's Emmy-winning performance as the mordant Southern neighbor Constance Langdon. The series pioneered a format where each season would tell a complete, self-contained horror narrative with many of the same actors in different roles — a structure that allowed continuous creative reinvention without narrative fatigue. Lange won the Emmy for Outstanding Supporting Actress in a Drama Series for Season 1, beginning a dominant awards run for the show.

The series drew 3.2 million viewers for its premiere, making it FX's most-watched series debut to that point. Its willingness to reference historical atrocities, incorporate psychosexual horror, and embrace camp aesthetics made it a distinctive presence in an era otherwise dominated by gritty realism.

### The Killing (AMC, April 3 – June 19, 2011)

AMC's American adaptation of Danish crime drama *Forbrydelsen* starred Mireille Enos as Seattle detective Sarah Lund and Joel Kinnaman as her partner. Though the show generated controversy for its season finale (which failed to resolve the central murder mystery despite 13 episodes), it became a cultural touchpoint for American audiences discovering Scandinavian noir aesthetics: atmospheric, slow-burn, character-driven crime drama that prioritized psychological depth over procedural action.

### Downton Abbey (PBS, January 9, 2011 — U.S. premiere)

ITV's British period drama about the Crawley family and their servants (set 1912–1926) debuted in the U.S. on PBS's *Masterpiece* on January 9, 2011, and quickly became the channel's most-watched drama in decades. At its American peak in the 2012–13 season, *Downton Abbey* drew 10 million viewers per episode to PBS — a remarkable figure for a foreign, subtlety-driven period drama. In 2011, its first U.S. season demonstrated that international television, particularly British period dramas, could achieve mainstream American success.

### Big Love, Season 5 (HBO, January 16 – March 20, 2011)

HBO's polygamy drama concluded its five-season run in March 2011 with the death of protagonist Bill Henrickson. The series, which followed a Utah fundamentalist Mormon family navigating mainstream American life, had been a modest ratings success throughout its run. Its conclusion freed HBO's Sunday-night prestige slot that *Game of Thrones* would soon occupy.

### Mad Men, Season 4 Aftermath and Hiatus

*Mad Men* had concluded its critically acclaimed Season 4 in October 2010, and creator Matthew Weiner engaged in a protracted and contentious contract renegotiation with AMC throughout 2011. The network demanded cast cuts and episode-length reductions; Weiner resisted, and the show did not return until March 2012 with Season 5 — a 17-month hiatus that generated considerable press attention. The dispute illustrated the growing power of showrunner-auteurs in the prestige television era and AMC's difficult position as a network investing heavily in quality drama while remaining smaller than HBO.

### The Emmy Awards (September 18, 2011)

The 63rd Primetime Emmy Awards highlighted the prestige cable drama's dominance. Notable winners included:
- **Outstanding Drama Series**: *Mad Men* (for Season 4, its fourth consecutive win in the category)
- **Outstanding Comedy Series**: *Modern Family* (its second consecutive win)
- **Outstanding Lead Actor, Drama**: Kyle Chandler, *Friday Night Lights*
- **Outstanding Lead Actress, Drama**: Julianna Margulies, *The Good Wife*
- *Game of Thrones* won two awards in its first year of eligibility, an unusual achievement for a freshman series

## The Streaming Inflection Point

Netflix entered 2011 with approximately 20 million U.S. streaming subscribers and 12 million DVD subscribers. The year proved turbulent: in July, CEO Reed Hastings announced a 60% price increase for customers who wanted both DVD and streaming plans, and in September he announced the disastrous "Qwikster" plan to spin off the DVD business into a separate company under a separate name. The backlash was immediate and severe — Netflix lost approximately 800,000 subscribers in Q3 2011, and its stock fell nearly 75% from its July peak.

The Qwikster plan was abandoned within three weeks, but the episode revealed the tensions in Netflix's transition from disc-by-mail to pure streaming. Despite the debacle, Netflix closed 2011 with approximately 21.7 million streaming subscribers, and the company's long-term bet on original content — confirmed when it committed $100 million to produce two seasons of *House of Cards* in March 2011 — would validate the strategy.

## Significance

2011 represented a decisive inflection point in television history, distinguishable from earlier prestige TV eras by scale: *Game of Thrones* was not merely a critical success but a global cultural event. Its debut established that television could command narrative ambition — budgets, world-building scope, and audience investment — previously associated only with major film franchises.

The cable/broadcast divide deepened measurably. In 2011, no broadcast network drama was nominated for the Outstanding Drama Series Emmy; all nominees (including *Mad Men*, *Dexter*, *The Good Wife*, *Friday Night Lights*, and *Game of Thrones*) came from cable or premium cable. This represented a near-complete reversal of the Emmy landscape from a decade prior, when broadcast dramas had dominated.

The year also illustrates how streaming's dominance was not inevitable but the result of specific bets: Netflix's $100 million commitment to *House of Cards* and Disney/ESPN's early streaming experiments were infrastructure investments that would only pay off years later. In 2011 streaming remained subordinate to cable in cultural conversation.
